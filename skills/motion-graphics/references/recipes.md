# Recipes and devices

Ready-made moves. The patterns are whole scene structures chosen by the viewer's
takeaway; the devices are the recurring small moves that give a pattern its voice. Pick a
pattern first, then fit devices to it.

Self-contained scene recipes. Choose one by the viewer's takeaway, then adapt its layout, assets, palette and tempo to the brief. None of them requires a reference video — when the user supplies one, use it to pick the pattern and set the look, not to replace the structure.

Timings are starting points measured from shipped films, not fixed templates. Combine a pattern here with the devices in [recipes.md](recipes.md) — the pattern sets the structure, the devices give it a voice.

## Feature demonstration

For a release whose improvements are easier to show than to explain.

1. Open on a short verb or benefit, revealed by a mask or directional move, settling sharply.
2. Bring in one real screen and keep it as the demonstration surface for the whole film.
3. Per benefit: name it briefly, perform the action, hold the visible change. Reuse the same surface so each change reads as a comparison rather than a new subject.
4. Alternate screen detail with wider context, carrying an anchor position or one object through the transition.
5. Finish on a sourced result and a readable action.

Cadence: 0.3–0.6s transitions, 1–3s demonstration holds, longer for dense comparisons. Keep background motion quieter than the control being used.

## Illustrated relationship

For a product that changes a relationship, workflow, or invisible system.

1. Introduce two or three recognisable symbols — person, task, tool, result.
2. Show the problem spatially: disconnected nodes, a blocked route, a queue backing up, a state nobody is watching.
3. Introduce the product as the connection or the intervention, and animate the changed relationship.
4. Ground the metaphor with one real UI detail or output.
5. Resolve into a stable useful state, then the product and the action.

Hold character proportions, stroke weight, and palette constant throughout. Move connected objects as a group during camera travel. Use expression and secondary motion only where they clarify the story — a mascot that emotes through an explanation competes with it.

## Device showcase

For a product whose presentation, physical geometry, or on-screen interaction is the point.

1. Establish device and environment wide.
2. Move toward the screen region the claim is about; animate real screen content on a dedicated mapped surface.
3. Reduce camera movement during the reading hold.
4. Transition through a shared device pose or screen element into the next context.
5. Pull back to a clean product silhouette and the closing action.

Drive camera position, target, and device pose from time. Inspect screen fit, corner mask, notch, occlusion, and perspective at the most extreme camera angle, not the easiest one. Lighting and reflection should reveal geometry without obscuring the interface, and a continuously orbiting unreadable screen is worse than a static legible one.

## Role and capability reveal

For one product that serves several roles or performs a sequence of different operations.

Open on a stable phrase with a cycling role or verb slot. Run the label list behind a clipped viewport so the selected label settles on the baseline while the others stay secondary. Then transition the selected label into its real action: typing for input, highlighted evidence for research, assembled components for design, a result view for completion.

Keep palette and anchors constant while the action language varies, and exit the selector once the idea has landed. A succession of large words is not proof; each role needs one real thing happening.

## Task completion

For a product best explained by one job done well. The most forgiving structure when the feature set is small, and the most convincing for anything agentic.

Request → acknowledgement → processing → useful artefact → outcome. A prompt bubble becomes a progress container, then a result card; outgoing text leaves before the container's new geometry would clip it. Move the camera to the result once it exists.

A mascot introduces or reacts to the task; it never interrupts the result. Show integrations only after the task has established why they matter. Keep the loading state short enough to keep momentum and long enough for cause and effect to read — usually 0.6–1.2s.

## Abstract to interface

For an abstract problem with a tangible product solution.

1. Create tokens for the problem: links, files, notes, tasks, messages.
2. Accumulate or arrange them until the scale or the disorder is legible.
3. Pick one as the anchor; move the rest into the rows, cards, and controls of the real interface.
4. Demonstrate one real interaction and its outcome.
5. Return the tokens as a restrained brand composition for the CTA.

Match anchor position, scale, and shape across the transformation — that continuity is the whole trick. Keep irrelevant tokens low contrast so they do not compete with the headline, and give each token a deterministic per-token delay and trajectory rather than a random one.

## Technical proof

For an audience that buys on evidence.

Open with a short media-card or diagram formation, then settle into the actual query or workflow. Show the submitted input, the relevant processing state, and the generated artefact. Move closer to the useful table, code change, or answer and hold it long enough to be read. Restore context before the next task.

Separate decorative surfaces from readable evidence: a perspective card formation can move fast, an important answer needs a stable plane and a slow hold. Do not keep a cursor looping or fake typing after the answer is already on screen.

## Vertical explanation

For narration explaining a concept over time. See [other-formats.md](other-formats.md) for the frame zones and caption craft.

Reserve an upper visual stage and a stable caption region beneath it. Introduce a few tokens, arrange them around the narrated question, draw connectors as each relationship is explained, then reveal a screenshot or output that proves the point. Replace the hero when the idea changes; never move the captions.

Drive word emphasis from aligned narration timestamps. Reveal tokens in semantic groups, not one per spoken word. Test the longest caption and the densest diagram at 360 px wide.

## Numbered listicle

For several distinct items sharing one promise.

Give every item the same four states: ordinal and name → benefit → diagram or demonstration → takeaway. Keep the ordinal's anchor, typography, and transition family identical across items so the viewer tracks progress without a progress bar; vary the hero inside each item so it does not feel mechanical.

Use a brief palette or mask reset between chapters. End on the list's practical payoff and one action. For a repeatable series, parameterise copy, assets, and narration while sharing the layout components, and do not force every demonstration into an identical duration.

## Technique showreel

For when motion craft or creative identity is the subject.

Sequence contrasting technique families: kinetic type, an easing comparison, a continuous shape morph, a patterned field, spatial or point geometry, and a final identity lockup. Carry a palette, motif, or anchor between them so it reads as authored rather than sampled.

This is the one pattern where corner metadata, framing marks, and deliberately mixed easing families belong — the techniques are the subject, so showing several easing behaviours side by side is the content. Include arrival holds; constant rapid cutting conceals craft instead of demonstrating it.

## Chaptered release

For a broad announcement covering several capabilities or audiences.

Group related benefits into chapters. Each chapter opens with a recognition cue, shows real UI or a useful result, and closes on a payoff. Alternate dense evidence with quieter reset shots, and keep typography, accent, and layout rules constant even as the media varies.

A second product reveal needs its own setup and a reason to follow the first. Compress repeated waiting states, never the proof the viewer needs. Judge timing per chapter instead of imposing one shot length across the film — this is the structure most often ruined by compressing a wide release into a catalogue of unreadable thumbnails.

## Continuous UI morph

For a product whose story is its interface. One container, never cut.

A single element moves through 8–12 states, changing size, radius, and fill while its content swaps behind a short blur. A cursor causes every change with a real click or hover, and the camera never cuts, so the object is never lost.

A measured state list from a shipped 14-second square loop: `Generate` button → loading spinner → success check → waveform pill → media player card → scrub bar → volume slider → toggle → segmented control → chart card with a tooltip → search field → command palette with results → confirmation pill → back to `Generate`. Fourteen states in fourteen seconds, one state per beat, the last frame identical to the first.

Note what the list has in common: every state is a real control that a real product would have, and each one is a plausible *next* state of the one before it. A list that jumps between unrelated widgets reads as a component gallery, not a product.

Animate the container's leading and trailing edges on different spring stiffnesses so it stretches as it moves rather than sliding rigidly; that one detail is what separates this from a slideshow.

**The colourful variation:** give each state its own full-bleed canvas colour, so the background changes with every morph while a constant set of soft background motifs — large circles, arcs — persists underneath. A measured loop runs pink, purple, cyan, blue, orange, green, yellow, red-orange and back to pink across thirteen states. It works because only two things change per beat, the container and the hue, while everything else holds; and it only works if the container stays white or near-black throughout, so it reads as one object travelling through environments rather than a new card each time.

Square is the natural format here — the container is centred and the surrounding canvas is plain, so 1:1 loses nothing and posts everywhere. Content enters after the morph starts and leaves before the next one begins. The last frame must equal the first, cursor position and velocity included.

# Signature devices


The recurring moves in shipped 2026 launch films, with implementations. These are what separates a film that looks designed from one that looks assembled, and most of them cost very little.

## Typed text with a caret

The single most common device in current launch films, and the cheapest to implement. It instantly reads as a person using the product rather than a slide appearing.

Type the headline rather than fading it in. Use a visible caret: solid while characters land, blinking once typing pauses. 20–30 characters per second reads as confident; below 15 feels slow, above 40 is unreadable.

```js
// Pure function of time: the character count is derived from t, never incremented.
export function typed(g, text, x, y, t, { cps = 26, start = 0, caret = '|', blink = 0.53, hold = 0.6 } = {}) {
  const el = Math.max(0, t - start);
  const n = Math.min(text.length, Math.floor(el * cps));
  const shown = text.slice(0, n);
  g.fillText(shown, x, y);
  const done = n >= text.length;
  const past = el - text.length / cps;
  if (done && past > hold) return { done: true, width: g.measureText(shown).width };
  const on = !done || (Math.floor(past / blink) % 2 === 0);   // blink only after the pause
  if (on) g.fillText(caret, x + g.measureText(shown).width, y);
  return { done, width: g.measureText(shown).width };
}
```

Left-align typed text. Centring it makes the whole line shift left as it grows, which is the giveaway that it is an effect rather than someone typing.

Two lines with the second dimmer is a strong pairing — the claim, then the qualifier: `steer it now.` / `or queue it for later.` Type the first, pause, type the second.

For a terminal or code product, use a monospace face and a block caret (`▌`) instead of a bar.

## Inline chips inside a sentence

A sentence with real product UI embedded in the running type — `Docs [📄] and [🤖] agents in one workspace.` It appears in almost every strong launch film of the last year, and it works because it fuses the claim and the proof into one object instead of cutting between them.

```js
// parts: ['Docs ', {chip:'page'}, ' and ', {chip:'bot'}, ' agents']
export function runWithChips(g, parts, x, y, { size = 48, gap = 10, draw }) {
  let cx = x;
  for (const p of parts) {
    if (typeof p === 'string') { g.fillText(p, cx, y); cx += g.measureText(p).width; }
    else { const w = size * (p.w ?? 1.9); draw(p, cx + gap, y - size * 0.78, w, size * 1.05); cx += w + gap * 2; }
  }
  return cx - x;      // total width — measure first, then centre the whole run
}
```

Measure the full run before drawing so the line can be centred as one object. Spring each chip in slightly after the word before it, so the sentence assembles left to right rather than appearing complete.

Keep chips at cap height, vertically centred on the baseline, with the same radius as the product's real UI. A chip taller than the line breaks the reading rhythm.

## The word slot

A stable phrase with one word swapping: `Built for ___` cycling teams, developers, agents. Or a role list where the selected item settles and the rest stay secondary.

Hold the fixed words completely still. Only the slot moves, clipped to its own viewport so words enter and leave without disturbing the line. Measure the widest variant and reserve that width, or the sentence reflows on every swap and the stillness is lost.

Three or four variants, roughly 0.4–0.6s each. Exit the device once the idea has landed; a slot that keeps cycling after the point is made becomes wallpaper.

## A stable caption region

For a product whose interface is the story, give the whole frame to the real UI and put every claim in one fixed region — lower left works well — in a consistent size and weight for the entire film.

The viewer learns where the words appear and stops hunting for them, which means the UI can change completely between shots without the film feeling discontinuous. Two short lowercase lines, the second dimmer, with one accent colour on the load-bearing word.

This is the opposite of alternating full-screen type cards with product shots, and it is better whenever the product is dense and worth looking at.

## A travelling object

One object that persists across cuts — a cursor, a mascot, a paper plane, a single card — carrying the eye from shot to shot. It is the cheapest continuity device there is, and it turns a sequence of scenes into one film.

Keep its position continuous across the cut: it leaves the old shot where it enters the new one. Give it a consistent scale and let it lead the camera rather than follow it. It should cause things, not just be present — arriving at a control before the control responds.

## Radial and arc formations

Media cards, avatars, or icons arranged on an arc or ring, rotating slowly, with one item pulled forward as the subject. Good for "many things, one system" moments — a knowledge store, an integration set, a content library.

Place items on the arc by index, not randomly, and give each a deterministic per-item delay. Keep the ring's rotation slow — under about 8 degrees per second — or the items become unreadable. Push the focused item toward the camera and dim the rest rather than stopping the motion.

## Dissolves with texture

Rather than a plain crossfade, dissolve through a texture: a halftone dot field, a scatter of small squares, a grain wash. The outgoing shot breaks into the texture and the incoming shot reassembles from it.

Drive it from a seeded hash per particle so it stays deterministic, and keep it to 0.3–0.5s. Use it two or three times in a film at most, on act boundaries — as an every-cut transition it becomes a screensaver.

A vignette iris — darkening inward to a circle and opening into the next shot — works well for the single transition into a final lockup.

## The closing line that reframes

The strongest launch films end on a sentence that reinterprets everything before it, rather than naming the product again: *You built a system.* The viewer has just watched a sequence of features, and one line turns it into an outcome they achieved.

Write it as something true about the viewer, not about the product. Hold it long enough to read twice, then resolve to the lockup. A film that ends on a feature list ends; a film that ends on a reframe lands.
